Interview with a Patient Care Technician

Interview Information

For this project I interviewed Vitalii Prolinskyi, a Patient Care Technician at Fresenius Kidney Care. He is a young professional in the medical field and he shared with me his experience of getting the job and routine of his everyday life at work.

Name of Interviewer Anya Prolinska
Name of Interviewee Vitalii Prolinskyi
Occupation / Specialty / Facility Patient Care Technician, Fresenius Kidney Care, San Antonio, Texas

Prior to Interview

I think working in a dialysis center is a strong place to start because it teaches you a lot about patient care, kidney disease, blood pressure, diabetes, cardiovascular problems, and how chronic illness affects people every day. I also like that nephrology connects with cardiology because so many dialysis patients have heart and blood pressure problems, too.

My biggest strength working at the dialysis center, just like Vitalii, probably would be that I am patient and willing to learn from the bottom up. I do not expect to know everything right away, and I do not rely on my previous experience working or studying in the healthcare field. My biggest weakness is that I sometimes overthink. The most rewarding part would be helping patients feel seen, safe, and respected. The hardest part would probably be seeing the same patients struggle with serious long-term illness and knowing that dialysis is physically and emotionally hard for them.

Interview Questions

Why did you decide to be a Patient Care Technician?

I wanted actual hands-on experience in healthcare, not just classroom knowledge. I graduated from San Antonio College as a pre-nursing student with an Associate of Applied Science in biology, and now I am planning on applying to a nursing program. I knew that if I wanted to become a good nurse, I needed to understand patient care from the basic level first.

Nephrology is very connected to the whole body. It affects blood pressure, electrolytes, fluid balance, the heart, and the patient’s whole life. I also had the chance to shadow and learn from Dr. Reyes, the doctor I interviewed for my 2402 A&P class, and that helped me see how close nephrology and cardiology are. He showed me that you cannot just look at one organ system by itself. Everything is connected.

What is the most rewarding part of your job?

Getting to know the patients and seeing that they trust you. A lot of dialysis patients come in several times a week, so you see them very often. You learn their routines, their personalities, what makes them nervous, and what helps them feel comfortable.

They are sitting there for hours, and many of them are tired or not feeling well. Being kind, calm, and careful can change their whole treatment experience. I like knowing that I can make the process feel a little less scary or stressful for them. Sometimes funny situations happen, we are chatting and joking around, and it is nice for me to see that they are not stressing as much as people usually do when doctors or nurses are present and the hospital atmosphere feels depressing.

What is the hardest part of your job?

Seeing how much chronic illness affects people. Dialysis is not just a quick appointment. It becomes part of the patient’s life. Some patients are weak, some are frustrated, some are scared, and some have been doing treatments for years.

It can also be hard because you have to stay focused the whole time. You are dealing with blood, machines, blood pressure changes, and patients who can suddenly feel bad during treatment. You cannot be careless. Even if the day is busy, you still have to pay attention to small details. Emotionally, it is hard when you build relationships with patients and then see them decline or have complications.

Is your job stressful? How do you handle the stress and demands of your day?

Yes, definitely. I am glad I do not have as much responsibility over patients as nurses or my proctors. Dialysis has a routine, but things can change fast. A patient’s blood pressure can drop, someone can feel dizzy or nauseous, the machine can alarm, or a patient can become anxious.

I need to follow the routine checkups on every patient every 20 minutes, and good time management is a must. Or else, everything might go wrong and cause a chain reaction. I handle the stress by trying to stay calm, following the steps, and asking questions when I need help.

What is your greatest skill as a Patient Care Technician?

My greatest skill is probably being observant and patient with people. You have to notice small changes. A patient might look more tired than usual, their blood pressure might be different, or they might say something small like, “I feel weird.” Those little signs can matter.

I also think I am good at making patients feel respected. I know that patients do not want to feel like they are just another chair or another treatment. They want to feel seen and treated like a person.

What is your greatest weakness as a Patient Care Technician?

I can be too hard on myself. I realize that, but I cannot do much. Like I said, I am an overthinker. Since I am trying to learn and prepare for nursing, I sometimes feel like I should already understand everything perfectly. But healthcare does not work like that. Life does not work like that.

I am working on being more confident while still being careful. I do not want to act overconfident, but I also do not want to doubt myself too much. I think that balance comes with practice and time.

What are the best and worst things about the place you work?

The best thing is that dialysis gives you a lot of direct patient experience. You are not just watching from far away. You are actually involved in care, and you learn how patients with kidney disease live day to day. I also like that the work connects to important topics like blood pressure, fluid overload, diabetes, heart problems, and lab values.

The hardest thing is that the pace can be demanding. There is a lot to do, and patients need consistent attention. The schedule is pretty hard too, with 12-hour shifts that start at 4 a.m. It can also be emotionally heavy because many patients are dealing with serious long-term conditions. You have to be professional, but you also cannot be cold. You need compassion and discipline at the same time.

What does a typical day at work look like for you?

A typical day starts at around 3 a.m. with preparing machines and units for patients. A part of my job is to make sure all the filters are properly maintained, cleaned, and functioning overall. Patients come in for treatment later, and we check things like weight, blood pressure, and how they are feeling.

Then we help with getting them ready for dialysis and monitor them during treatment. We keep an eye on the machines, check the amount of liquid that needs to be filtered based on the vitals, the patient’s feelings, and, of course, the doctor’s and nurses’ prescriptions. Some patients need reassurance, some need help adjusting, and some just want someone to check on them. After treatment, we help with ending the session, making sure the patient is stable, and cleaning and resetting the area.

What would your dream job be?

My dream job would be to become a nurse and maybe work in a specialty connected to nephrology, cardiology, or critical care. When I was shadowing and learning from Dr. Reyes, I started seeing how interesting that connection is. It is not a super extreme adrenaline rush-driven specialization, but it is a good middle part between interesting clinical cases, routine, high pay, and demand.

If you had to start all over again, would you choose the same career? Why?

Yes, I think I would. Healthcare is challenging, but it is also meaningful. It is respectable. This job gives me real responsibility and real patient interaction. It teaches humility. You learn that patient care is not only about big medical decisions. It is also about safety, communication, patience, and consistency.

Post-Interview Reflection

The most unexpected thing I learned from this interview was how much a Patient Care Technician in a dialysis center actually has to understand about the patient’s whole condition. Before the interview, I thought this job was mostly about helping with basic care and assisting during dialysis treatments. After hearing Vitalii’s answers, I realized that dialysis care is connected to many body systems, especially the urinary, cardiovascular, and endocrine systems. Patients who need dialysis often have kidney failure, blood pressure problems, diabetes, fluid imbalance, and heart-related risks. So even though the Patient Care Technician is not the provider, they still have to observe carefully and understand what changes might be important.

His answers compared to my first impression in a more serious way. I expected the job to be busy, but I did not realize how emotionally demanding it could be. Seeing the same patients multiple times a week means that workers build relationships with them. That can make the job rewarding, but also hard when patients are struggling or getting worse.

My attitude toward this career option became more respectful. I can see how working as a Patient Care Technician is a strong foundation for nursing because it teaches patient communication, routine care, observation, and responsibility. I also liked how Vitalii talked about learning “from the bottom up.” That made the career feel realistic.

To become a better candidate in this field, I think I would need to improve my confidence, clinical vocabulary, time management, and ability to stay calm under pressure. This interview showed me that even entry-level healthcare roles can teach important lessons if a person is serious about learning.
